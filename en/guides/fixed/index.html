What a same-amount savings chart is

You put in the same amount every time. $5 a day means $5 every day. $25 a week means $25 every week. No step is heavier than any other, which is what sets it apart from the 52 week chart, where the amount moves every single week.

The clearest advantage is that you always know what to set aside. No chart to check, no arithmetic, one number to remember. And because it is predictable, it is the easiest method of the four to put on an automatic transfer.

The amount most people start at is $5 a day, because it goes missing without being felt, and a year of it comes to $1,825. And $10 a day lands on exactly $1,000 after 100 days, which is the figure people set as a first goal more than any other.

How much a day to reach $1,000?

The arithmetic backwards is easy — divide the target by the number of payments and round up to a clean number. The total lands a little over, which beats coming up short at the end. This table has it worked out already.

Charts for $5,000 and $10,000

Targets in the thousands work exactly the same way. All that changes is that the daily amount climbs high enough to need real planning.

$5,000 in a year is $14 a day. $10,000 in a year is $28 a day.

If the per-day number starts to feel uncomfortable, switch to weekly or monthly. The money is the same; it is only easier to think about, and easier to tie to the day you get paid. And if it still feels heavy, that means the timeline should stretch, not that the amount should be forced.

Pick daily if…Money comes in every day or nearly every day — a shop, day work, driving deliveries. Small amounts go unfelt, and doing it every day is what turns it into a habit.
Pick weekly if…You want it tied to a day off, or the day you go to the bank. Once a week is easier to remember, and missing one does not mean missing the month.
Pick monthly if…Your pay lands on the same day every month. Set the transfer for payday and you do not think about it again until the next one.

What it is good at, what it costs you, and who it suits

Every method has a price. This one does too. Both sides are here so you can choose before you start, rather than find out in month three and quit.

Good at
  • You always know what to set aside. One number to remember
  • You can work the total out in your head: amount per step times number of steps
  • It goes on an automatic transfer, because the amount never changes
  • The easiest of the four to keep going. No step is unusually heavy
  • Stretch or shorten the timeline without redoing the chart
Costs you
  • It does not adapt at all. In a tight month the only options are skip or rewrite
  • A good month still saves the same, so you save less than you could have
  • It is dull. None of the rise and fall that makes the 52 week chart interesting
  • Set the amount too high and you find out in month two, which is already late
Suits you if
  • Your pay lands on the same day every month
  • You want to set a transfer and forget about it
  • You are saving for the first time and do not want anything complicated
  • You have quit before because the late payments got too heavy
  • You are saving alongside someone else, where an equal amount is easier to agree on
Not for you if
  • Your income changes from month to month
  • You run a shop or take work by the job, with no reliable payday
  • You want to save hard at the start because you are afraid of quitting
  • You want the amount to grow as your income grows

The first three on the bottom right are what the other methods are for — an uneven income wants a share of what comes in, and a fear of quitting halfway wants a little less each time. Both are linked at the foot of this page.

The research: why this method fears a gap most

This method makes the prettiest chart to put on a wall, because every box is the same size. Which also means an unticked box is the thing you see first. There is research on exactly that.

Silverman and Barasch, 2023 · Journal of Consumer Research
Seven studies on what people do when a streak in a record breaks — keep going, or stop.

They found that once the record shows the streak is broken, people continue less, and the effect depends on how it looks in the record rather than on what the person actually did. It weakens when the broken record can be repaired.

Sharif and Shu, 2019 · Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes
A test of “emergency reserves” — building permission to miss into a goal — measuring how many people abandoned it entirely.
4.1%
of people whose goal allowed misses gave up entirely
Experiment 4a · 402 participants
18–23%
of people whose goal allowed none gave up entirely
Comparison groups in the same experiment

Decide before you start that misses are allowed and that you will tick them in later. Do not let an empty box be the thing that decides whether you keep going.

WHAT THESE STUDIES DO NOT SHOW

Read those numbers knowing this

  • Neither study looked at saving money. They studied step counts and games in a lab. That is why I write “research on goals” and not “research on saving”.

When you miss a payment

You missed one or twoDo not start over. The total matters more than the order. Put the money in when you have it and tick the boxes you left empty.
The amount was too high from the startLower it and stretch the timeline. The target does not change, it just arrives later. That beats quitting and arriving at zero.
This month is genuinely tightSkip one on purpose and write it down. A deliberate skip is not the same as forgetting, because you are still the one deciding.
Tight several months runningA fixed chart no longer matches the rhythm of your money. A share of what comes in moves with your income instead.

The amount I would start with — $500 in 100 days

If you do not know what number to start at, I would say $5 a day for 100 days, which comes to $500. Small enough to start today without thinking about it, and short enough to prove to yourself that you finish things, before you take on anything bigger.

If you get through those first 100 days, treat the $500 as evidence that you can, and move up to $10 or $20 a day for the next round.
